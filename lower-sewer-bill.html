Sewer bill savings guide

How to lower your sewer bill in 2026

A sewer bill can be confusing because the charge is not always based on a separate sewer meter. Depending on the utility, part of the bill may be tied to water use, a seasonal average, fixed service charges or a combination of methods. Start by identifying the formula before trying to cut the total.

Review My Bill

1. Find out how your utility calculates sewer charges

Look for a line labeled sewer, wastewater, sanitary sewer, treatment or similar wording. Then check whether the statement shows a billed volume, a fixed monthly charge, a rate per unit or a seasonal average.

Some utilities base wastewater charges partly on measured water consumption because most indoor water eventually enters the sewer system. Others use fixed charges or a winter-average method. The exact rule is local, so verify the formula shown on your utility's tariff or customer-service page.

2. Compare billing days and billed volume

Put the current bill next to a previous bill. Compare the number of billing days, total water use, any sewer usage units and the sewer rate. A longer billing period can raise the total even when daily household use is similar.

If the sewer charge jumped but water use did not, focus on rate changes, fixed charges, adjustments or a change in the utility's averaging method.

3. Check whether a winter average is being used

Some utilities estimate sewer use from water consumption during selected winter months, when outdoor irrigation is usually lower. If your provider uses this method, unusually high winter water use can affect later sewer charges.

Ask the utility which months are used, whether the average is recalculated each year and whether leaks or verified unusual events can be reviewed under its adjustment policy.

4. Look for leaks before trying to save elsewhere

If sewer charges are tied to water consumption, an unnoticed leak can increase both the water and sewer portions of the statement. Running toilets, dripping fixtures, irrigation leaks and hidden plumbing leaks can all create continuous use.

The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency's WaterSense program recommends checking for leaks and monitoring unexpected water use. If your utility provides hourly or daily data, look for usage during overnight periods when no water should be running.

EPA WaterSense leak guidance provides a practical starting point.

5. Separate fixed charges from usage-based charges

Reducing water use will not necessarily reduce every sewer line item. Customer charges, infrastructure charges, minimum bills and other fixed fees may remain the same even when consumption falls.

That is why percentage savings on water use do not always translate into the same percentage reduction on the sewer bill. Identify which charges are actually variable before estimating savings.

6. Review irrigation and outdoor water rules

In some areas, outdoor water use can affect the sewer calculation if all consumption is measured through one meter. Other utilities offer irrigation meters, seasonal credits or other arrangements that separate qualifying outdoor use from wastewater billing.

Do not assume these programs exist everywhere. Check the utility's published rules and compare any meter or program fee with the potential savings before making a change.

7. Check estimated or corrected meter readings

If the water meter reading was estimated and later corrected, both water and sewer charges may move at the same time. Compare the reading type and start/end meter values on consecutive statements.

Our estimated meter reading guide explains how to spot a catch-up adjustment.

8. Ask about leak-adjustment policies

Some utilities offer limited bill adjustments after a documented leak is repaired, while others do not. If a confirmed leak caused the spike, keep the repair invoice or other proof and ask whether the account qualifies for review.

Policies can differ by leak type, timing, account history and local rules, so treat this as a question for the provider rather than an automatic credit.

Quick sewer-bill checklist

  • Identify whether the charge is fixed, usage-based or averaged.
  • Compare billing days and water consumption.
  • Compare the sewer rate and fixed service charges.
  • Check for winter-average or seasonal calculations.
  • Look for continuous water use or leaks.
  • Check actual versus estimated meter readings.
  • Review outdoor-water and irrigation rules.
  • Ask whether repaired leaks qualify for an adjustment.
