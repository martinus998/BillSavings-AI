Utility bill diagnosis

Why is my utility bill so high?

A high electric, gas or water bill usually comes from one of a few measurable changes: more usage, a higher rate, more billing days, a different meter reading or new fixed charges. Compare those pieces before assuming the entire increase came from consumption.

Review My Utility Bill

Start with this 5-minute comparison

1. Compare the total

Write down the current total and the previous total so you know the exact dollar difference you are trying to explain.

2. Compare billing days

A bill covering more days can be higher even when daily use is nearly unchanged.

3. Compare daily usage

Divide total usage by billing days. This helps separate a longer billing cycle from a real increase in consumption.

4. Compare the rate and fixed charges

Look at the price per unit plus delivery, customer, service and other fixed lines.

The six parts most likely to explain a higher utility bill

Usage

Compare electricity, gas or water consumption with the previous bill and the same season last year when possible. If usage changed sharply, look for weather, heating and cooling, leaks, new appliances or household changes.

Rate per unit

Check whether the price per kilowatt-hour, therm, gallon or other unit changed. A rate increase can raise the total even when consumption barely moves.

Billing days

A longer cycle can make one bill look unusually high. Use our longer billing cycle checklist to compare daily usage instead of totals alone.

Delivery and fixed charges

Customer, delivery, distribution and service charges may change independently of usage. On electric bills, our electric delivery charge guide explains how delivery can differ from supply.

Meter reading

An estimated reading followed by a corrected or catch-up reading can shift usage into one statement. See our estimated meter reading guide.

Budget billing

Level-pay or budget-billing programs can recalculate the monthly payment after reviewing prior usage and account balance. See our budget billing adjustment guide.

If usage stayed similar but the bill increased

This is one of the most useful clues. When daily usage is close to the previous period but the total is higher, focus on the price side of the bill: rate per unit, delivery charges, customer charges, fuel adjustments, taxes, lost discounts and account fees.

If the statement shows a separate fuel or purchased-energy factor, review our fuel adjustment charge guide. If several unfamiliar lines appeared at once, use the utility bill fee guide.

If usage increased sharply

When daily consumption is clearly higher, the next question is what changed in the home. For electricity, consider heating or cooling demand, space heaters, electric water heating, pool equipment, EV charging or a new appliance. For natural gas, compare heating demand and hot-water use. For water, check irrigation, toilets, faucets and other possible leaks.

Use the category-specific guides for a deeper checklist: high electric bill, high gas bill, high water bill and high sewer bill.

New account? Separate recurring costs from setup charges

A first statement can include items that will not appear every month. Deposits, partial billing periods, account setup and activation-related charges can make the first bill look much higher than the ongoing monthly amount.

Check our utility deposit charge guide and first bill higher than expected checklist before treating the whole increase as a permanent price change.

What to do if the bill still looks wrong

Verify the reading

Check whether the statement says actual, estimated or corrected and compare the meter number when available.

Identify one changed line

Ask which exact usage, rate, fee or adjustment explains the difference from the previous statement.

Ask for the calculation

Have the provider explain how the disputed line was calculated and what period it covers.

Use the dispute process if needed

If the explanation does not match the statement, use the provider's formal billing-dispute process and keep copies of the bills and correspondence.

Build a simple bill history

Save at least a few consecutive statements and track total usage, billing days, rate per unit and total amount. A short history makes it easier to spot a seasonal pattern, a promotion ending, a rate change or an estimated-reading correction. It also gives you clearer evidence when you contact the provider.

Frequently asked questions

Can weather alone cause a big increase?

Yes. Heating and cooling demand can change significantly during very hot or cold periods, but compare daily usage and billing days before assuming weather is the only cause.

Are delivery charges optional?

Often they are required utility charges, although the structure varies by provider and location.

Can a longer billing cycle make the bill look high?

Yes. Compare usage per day rather than total usage alone to see whether the increase is mainly due to more days.

Does BillSavings AI dispute bills for me?

No. It helps organize supported statements and highlight changes worth reviewing with the provider.
