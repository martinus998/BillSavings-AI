Water bill savings guide

How to lower your water bill in 2026

A high water bill is easier to solve when you separate usage, billing-cycle changes, meter readings, rates and account fees. Start with the bill itself before changing habits or calling the utility.

Review My Bill

1. Compare this bill with the previous one

Look at the current and prior statement side by side. Compare total water usage, the number of days in the billing period, the meter-reading dates, the price per unit and fixed service charges. A higher total does not always mean your daily usage increased. A longer billing cycle or corrected reading can raise the total even when your household routine did not change much.

If the increase appeared suddenly, also use our high water bill checklist and the broader high utility bill guide.

2. Check for leaks before assuming the rate changed

Leaks are one of the most important things to rule out. The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency says the average household's leaks can waste more than 9,300 gallons of water each year. Toilets, faucets, irrigation systems, supply lines and hidden plumbing leaks can all contribute to unexpected usage.

One simple check is to stop using water for a period and watch whether the meter still moves. If your utility offers hourly or daily usage data, compare overnight or away-from-home periods for unexplained consumption. EPA WaterSense also provides consumer guidance on finding leaks and using leak-detection or flow-monitoring devices.

EPA WaterSense leak guidance can help with a basic inspection.

3. Convert the bill into daily usage

Divide the billed usage by the number of billing days. This gives you a daily figure that is easier to compare across months with different cycle lengths. If daily usage is nearly unchanged but the total bill is higher, focus on rates, fixed charges, sewer charges or billing adjustments rather than household consumption alone.

If daily usage itself jumped, think about irrigation, guests, seasonal outdoor use, a running toilet, a new appliance, a pool, plumbing work or a meter correction.

4. Separate water charges from sewer and fixed fees

Many statements combine several components: water usage, base service, sewer, infrastructure, stormwater or other local charges. Some are based on consumption and others are fixed. A reduction in water use may not reduce every line by the same amount.

If the sewer portion is the problem, see why a sewer bill may be high. For unfamiliar account lines, use our utility bill fee guide.

5. Check whether the meter reading was estimated or corrected

Estimated readings can create a catch-up bill when the utility later obtains an actual reading. Compare the current and previous reading type and the start/end meter values. If a corrected reading caused the increase, ask the utility to explain the adjustment and whether payment arrangements are available if the catch-up amount is unusually large.

Our estimated meter reading guide explains this pattern in more detail.

6. Review rate changes and billing dates

Utilities may change usage rates, tier thresholds or fixed service charges. Compare the unit price shown on the bill instead of relying only on the total. Also check whether the billing period contains more days than usual. A 35-day bill can look much higher than a 28-day bill even when daily usage is similar.

For this issue, see longer billing cycle high bill.

7. Reduce usage only after you know what is driving the bill

Once you know the increase really comes from usage, target the largest source first. Repair leaks, shorten unnecessary running-water time, run full loads in dishwashers and washing machines, review irrigation schedules and replace inefficient fixtures when it makes financial sense. The best savings action depends on where the water is actually going.

When available, compare your utility's online usage history before and after a change. That gives you evidence that the action worked instead of relying on guesswork.

A quick water-bill review checklist

  • Compare total usage with the previous bill.
  • Compare billing days and calculate daily usage.
  • Check whether the meter reading was actual, estimated or corrected.
  • Look for leaks and unexplained overnight usage.
  • Compare the rate per unit and any tier changes.
  • Separate water, sewer and fixed service charges.
  • Ask the utility to explain any new adjustment or unfamiliar fee.
